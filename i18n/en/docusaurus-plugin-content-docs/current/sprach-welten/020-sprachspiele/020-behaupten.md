<!-- # The Language Game of Asserting -->

<!--t src=73422a50-->
## Real-life examples

<!--t src=96aabf53-->
In everyday life we meet assertions at every turn:

<!--t src=f5115665-->
- "It will rain tomorrow", (I know the future)
- "This restaurant has the best pizza in town", (I know every pizza)
- "Inflation will fall next year", (I know the future of the stock market)
- "Cannabis is more dangerous than alcohol", (I am a drug expert)
- "People are selfish by nature." (I am a hobby anthropologist and sociologist)

<!--t src=663c38c0-->
Scientific theses, journalistic statements, political promises and everyday expressions of opinion, all of them operate within the language game of asserting.

<!--t src=3ce4fe61-->
## What it aims at

<!--t src=d2deba1b-->
Whoever asserts wants to present a state of affairs as true or correct. Asserting is thus a linguistic act by which we relate to the world, convey knowledge and influence other people &mdash; not seldom asserting precisely what serves our own interest.

<!--t src=c23528b9-->
Unlike asking or commanding, assertions raise a **claim to truth**: they purport that things actually are as asserted. The goal can vary &mdash; from the pure passing on of information, through convincing others, to reassuring oneself.

<!--t src=e9c44be0-->
## Success criteria

<!--t src=1d82b505-->
Whether an assertion is "successful" can be judged on three different levels, which should be kept apart:

<!--t src=9cbcb21f-->
**1. Does the linguistic act succeed?** (Is a seriously meant assertion made at all?)

<!--t src=752d1cb8-->
- The speaker means what he says and himself believes it to be true.
- He can, when asked, give reasons or evidence.
- The recipient understands that something is being asserted here &mdash; and not, say, asked, joked or commanded.

<!--t src=cfe285f0-->
**2. Is the assertion true?** (A separate question &mdash; an assertion remains an assertion even if it is false.)

<!--t src=3578eee0-->
- What is asserted corresponds to the facts, or is at least well grounded.

<!--t src=ef5dfb52-->
**3. Does it achieve the intended effect?** (What reaches the recipient is no longer in the speaker's hands.)

<!--t src=fc0cdf64-->
- The recipient regards the assertion as credible.
- It brings about what was intended (such as information or conviction).

<!--t src=4cfaffa7-->
These three levels often come apart: an assertion can succeed and still be false; it can be true and yet convince no one. Whoever thinks critically therefore asks separately:

<!--t src=29c6d45d-->
- 1. Is it seriously meant?
- 2. Is it true?
- 3. And why does it work?

<!--t src=b682f0ec-->
Which standards apply depends strongly on context: in scientific discourse the testing is stricter than in everyday life. What passes among friends ("That's the best film of all time") would be objected to in an academic work.

<!--t src=f899b6a7-->
## How it can go wrong

<!--t src=93f2b26b-->
An assertion can fail in two fundamentally different ways &mdash; and both have _nothing_ to do with whether what is asserted is true. (A false assertion is still an assertion; its falsity is a separate matter.)

<!--t src=9deef174-->
**The act does not properly come about at all** when:

<!--t src=633a2a15-->
- the assertion is too vague or ambiguous, so that it remains unclear _what_ is actually being asserted.
- the recipient does not recognize that anything is being asserted at all (for instance because it is misunderstood as a joke or a question).
- the social context makes the assertion appear inappropriate and it is therefore not taken seriously.

<!--t src=4593f713-->
**The act does succeed, but is insincere** when:

<!--t src=dff77165-->
- the asserter himself does not believe what he says (in the extreme case: the lie).
- he has no reasons or evidence whatsoever for his assertion and could give none.

<!--t src=b70445ed-->
To be distinguished from this is _credibility_: an assertion by a speaker who enjoys no trust or expertise does succeed &mdash; it is just less likely to be believed. Finally, especially tricky are assertions that rest on false premises or cannot be checked at all.

<!--t src=4775c5b9-->
## How it is abused

<!--t src=2ed53d94-->
Abuse can be captured precisely in the terms introduced above: the _form_ of the assertion is used, but the duty of sincerity and justification is deliberately violated. This shows on two levels.

<!--t src=159e2744-->
**Distortion of the truth content:**

<!--t src=bd54a17e-->
- Deliberate lies and invented facts
- Half-truths that omit important facts
- Assertions presented as facts although they are only opinions

<!--t src=0e0b9a79-->
**Strategic discourse tactics:**

<!--t src=b00b8e5e-->
- The spreading of rumours or conspiracy theories
- Countering criticism with a counter-accusation instead of answering it ("whataboutism")
- Flooding with so many assertions that careful checking becomes impossible ("firehose of falsehood")

<!--t src=3645329a-->
In politics and advertising, assertions are often deployed strategically to achieve certain effects, regardless of their truth content.

<!--t src=947d7d94-->
## Social role

<!--t src=c05c2f35-->
Asserting fulfils essential social functions:

<!--t src=bcf8ae6e-->
- It enables the exchange of knowledge and collective learning
- It serves social positioning and identity formation ("As a scientist, I assert...")
- It structures discourses and enables rationally conducted debates
- It creates shared realities and a common horizon of understanding
- It is fundamental to scientific, legal and political processes

<!--t src=7cca0d7a-->
In modern democratic societies, the ability to **examine assertions critically** and to make well-founded assertions of one's own is a central competence of mature citizens. At the same time, the social negotiation over which assertions count as credible and who may "assert" at all is a field of constant social contestation.

## Literature

<!--t src=2992c853-->
The analysis of speech acts in general, and of asserting as a linguistic act in particular, is treated among others in these relevant works:

<!--t src=0b029f7c-->
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein**, _Philosophical Investigations_ (1953; German _Philosophische Untersuchungen_). — Coins the concept of the **language game**: the meaning of an expression lies in its use, not in an abstract definition.
- **J. L. Austin**, _How to Do Things with Words_ (1962; German _Zur Theorie der Sprechakte_, Reclam 1972). — Founds **speech act theory** and distinguishes the locutionary, illocutionary and perlocutionary act, as well as the _felicity conditions_ (misfires vs. abuses).
- **John R. Searle**, _Speech Acts_ (1969; German _Sprechakte. Ein sprachphilosophischer Essay_, Suhrkamp 1971). — Systematizes the **felicity conditions** of asserting (preparatory, sincerity and essential condition) and the _direction of fit_ between word and world.
- **Robert B. Brandom**, _Making It Explicit_ (1994; German _Expressive Vernunft_, Suhrkamp 2000). — Understands asserting as a move in the **"game of giving and asking for reasons"**: whoever asserts undertakes a commitment for which reasons can be demanded.
