<!-- # The Language Game of Narrating -->

<!--t src=73422a50-->
## Real-life examples

<!--t src=f672fbe8-->
Narrating pervades our everyday life in countless forms: the grandmother reading fairy tales to her grandchildren; the friend recounting his holiday; the politician sketching a "story" about the future of the country; the patient describing his history of symptoms to the doctor; the news presenter reporting on events; the novel that carries us off into other worlds. Even our identity we construct narratively: "I am someone who..."

<!--t src=3ce4fe61-->
## What it aims at

<!--t src=683e61f0-->
The language game of narrating aims primarily at bringing experiences and events into a temporal and causal structure and thereby giving them meaning. Unlike mere asserting, it is less about isolated facts than about embedding them in a larger context. Narrating creates coherence, connects the unconnected and makes abstract concepts tangible through concrete scenarios. It conveys not only information but also meaning, emotion and values.

<!--t src=e9c44be0-->
## Success criteria

<!--t src=98759e8f-->
A narrative counts as successful when:

<!--t src=5e5d3d98-->
- It is comprehensible and followable for the listeners
- It shows an inner consistency and coherence
- It achieves the expected emotional effect (entertainment, comfort, surprise, etc.)
- It is felt to be appropriate and relevant in the given context
- It agrees with the conventions of the respective narrative format

<!--t src=a24dea44-->
The criteria vary strongly depending on the narrative context: in a scientific case study the precise sequence of facts is paramount, in a fairy tale the moral message, in an anecdote among friends the entertainment value.

<!--t src=f899b6a7-->
## How it can go wrong

<!--t src=07240941-->
Narrating can fail in various ways:

<!--t src=9a10750e-->
- The story is told too unstructured or confused
- Essential elements are forgotten or presented in the wrong order
- The narrator misses the appropriate tone for the situation
- The story is too long, too short or thematically unsuitable for the context
- The narrator loses himself in unimportant details and misjudges the core message
- The listeners can establish no connection to the narrative

<!--t src=c5388a65-->
It becomes especially problematic when the narrator blurs the difference between fiction and reality, or when important parts of the story are left out in order to create a false impression.

<!--t src=4775c5b9-->
## How it is abused

<!--t src=a55d04af-->
Narrating can be abused in many ways:

<!--t src=40b9fdcf-->
- By telling "narratives" that inadmissibly oversimplify complex matters
- By constructing enemy images and stereotypes in political or cultural narratives
- By selectively omitting facts that do not fit the desired narrative
- By telling stories meant to provoke emotional reactions in order to bypass rational reflection
- By spreading "anecdotal evidence" instead of systematic data
- By constructing conspiracy narratives that offer simple explanatory patterns for complex problems

<!--t src=361d8b26-->
A particularly powerful abuse is "gaslighting", in which a person's perception of reality is deliberately distorted through manipulative narratives.

<!--t src=947d7d94-->
## Social role

<!--t src=ffe11d1f-->
Narrating fulfils fundamental social functions:

<!--t src=fb00ff35-->
- It serves cultural transmission and the formation of tradition
- It creates collective identity and a sense of community
- It enables empathy by placing us into the experiences of others
- It conveys implicit social norms and values
- It offers emotional relief and the processing of experiences
- It creates shared interpretations of reality

<!--t src=5a64f363-->
Ultimately human societies are permeated by narratives &mdash; from the great cultural "meta-narratives" down to the small everyday stories. In the ability to tell and understand stories lies a key to human cooperation and cultural evolution. At the same time, critical distance from the narratives that surround and shape us remains a central task of reflective thinking.
